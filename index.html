(Possible?) FAQ

Why did I make this?

There is already plenty of documentation and learning materials for Web technologies, with varying levels of quality. Many are pretty good, better than what I could ever make, and always up-to-date.

Sometimes though, even though a page tells you everything there is to know about a particular concept, it doesn't tell you what you wanted to know.

As an example, take the MDN documentation for the start() method of the AudioScheduledSourceNode interface. The page says what it does, explains the parameters and the possible errors, and gives an example.

At the time of writing this, the page contains the following text (verbatim):

“Exceptions

InvalidStateNode DOMException

Thrown if the node has already been started. This error occurs even if the node is no longer running because of a prior call to stop().”

It says you can only call this method once or an exception is thrown.

It aknowledges the fact that a programmer might incorrectly assume that the node can be resumed after being stopped, and might try to call this method again after calling stop().

It doesn't tell you, however, that it can't be resumed.

It doesn't tell you that it can be, either.

It doesn't tell you how to achieve the effect of pausing and resuming, even if the implementation would involve different methods.

Instead, it leaves you to search for it on your own, with the possibility of wasting your time, or worse, to lose your faith in humanity in case you land on an old StackOverflow thread where everybody's telling you why your way of doing it is wrong.

It knows you want to do it. It just wants to see you suffer.


I'm not picking on MDN in particular. Every documentation for every technology I've ever seen does this, to one degree or another. Actually, MDN is one of the best in this regard. Yet, it still happens more often than I'd like.

Sometimes you want to learn something new. Other times, however, you just want to know how to do something.

So anyway, this is why I'm making this website.

P.S.: In order to find how to correctly add “smart quotes” to the above citation (without resorting to the Windows character map), I looked up “html quote entity” and “html smart quote entity” on Ecosia. The first five results for each query contained information about &quot;, mentioned the existence of other quote entities, yet did not say what they are nor gave links to other pages. One of the pages was called “Common HTML entities used for typography”, and it only talked about ASCII entities.

I then looked up the exact same search query (“html smart quote entity”) on Google, and the AI widget that they force you to use contained &ldquo; and &rdquo; in the first four lines of its answer. I didn't even need to click on it.

One tries to avoid it, but sometimes it feels like we deserve what we got.
